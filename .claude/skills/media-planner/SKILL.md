# Media Planner


Most pitch media plans are a list of channels and a pie chart. A real one answers three questions a client will ask and a scored tender will mark: where does the money go, how many people does that actually reach, and how will we know it worked. This skill is about being able to answer all three with numbers you can defend.


The rule that governs everything here: **never state a reach or frequency number you cannot show the working for.** An invented coverage figure is the fastest way to lose a media-literate room, and in a public tender it is worse than that.


## Step 1. Get the real budget


Work in net working media, not headline budget. Strip it back in this order and show the table.



Headline budget (what the brief says) minus VAT (Ireland 23%; headline / 1.23 if VAT inclusive) minus agency fees: strategy, creative, account, planning and buying, reporting minus production: film, audio, stills, versioning, build minus ad serving, verification, research and any tracker wave minus contingency (5% is normal, say so) = WORKING MEDIA. The only number that buys anybody's attention.



Two things to check in the brief that change this completely: whether the budget is VAT inclusive, and whether media spend sits inside it or alongside it. Get it wrong and every number after it is wrong.


State the working media figure as a percentage of the headline. Under 40% needs an explanation. Over 70% usually means production has been underpriced.


## Step 2. Size the audience


You cannot calculate reach without a universe. Get the real population, from a real source.


- Ireland: CSO Census and population estimates. Note that CSO publishes five-year bands, so an 18 to 34 total is arithmetic you do yourself. Say so when you show it.
- UK: ONS. Eurostat for EU comparisons.
- Media universes: TAM Ireland and Nielsen for TV, JNLR for radio, JNRS for print, Kantar and Ipsos for cross-media, the platforms' own planning tools for digital.


Write the universe down with its source before you plan anything. Everything downstream divides by it.


## Step 3. Choose channels from the job, not from habit


Match the channel to what the work has to do, and write the reason in one line each. If you cannot write the reason, cut the channel.


| The job | What tends to do it |
|---|---|
| Build mental availability, broad and cheap | Audio, broadcast or streaming. Outdoor. Online video. |
| Reach people who are actively looking | Paid search. It is the only channel that buys intent. |
| Reach people who will never look | Audio, social, programmatic, outdoor. Presence, not intent. |
| Frequency at low cost | Programmatic display, social, digital outdoor. |
| Credibility by association | Print, podcast host reads, partnerships, sponsorship. |
| A specific moment or place | Digital outdoor by daypart, transit, proximity, contextual. |
| Something people do | Search, social with a clear action, owned. |


Then check the plan against the audience's actual behaviour rather than the plan's convenience. If the data says half the audience never searches, a search-led plan reaches half the audience.


## Step 4. Reach and frequency, with the working shown


This is the part most pitch plans skip. Do the arithmetic, show it, and label it an estimate.


**The chain:**



Impressions      = (channel budget / CPM) x 1000 Gross impressions = sum across channels Average frequency = the planning assumption you choose, and state Net reach (people) = gross impressions / average frequency Reach %           = net reach / universe



**Where CPMs come from.** Use published rate cards, trade benchmarks (IAB Ireland, Core's annual outlook, WARC, eMarketer), or the platforms' own planners. Record the source and the date for every CPM. Prices move, so a CPM from two years ago is a guess wearing a number's clothes.


**Frequency.** Choose it deliberately and justify it. Three to five over a flight is a normal awareness assumption. For a sensitive category, excessive frequency is a harm, not just waste, so the cap is a planning decision and belongs on the page.


**The honesty rules.**


- Label every reach figure as an estimate, with the method beside it.
- Never add reach across channels. Duplication is real and ignoring it inflates everything. If you do not have a tool that de-duplicates, say you are showing gross impressions and single-channel reach only, and explain why.
- Digital reach on paper is not reach in the world. Apply a viewability assumption and say what it is.
- When a number cannot be estimated honestly, write "requires planning tool" rather than inventing it. A tender marker respects that more than a confident fiction.


**A worked example to copy the shape of.** 44,100 euro working media. 60% to audio at an 18 euro CPM, 40% to social and programmatic at an 8 euro CPM.



Audio:        26,460 / 18  x 1000 = 1.47m impressions Digital:      17,640 / 8   x 1000 = 2.21m impressions Gross:                             3.68m impressions Universe (adults 18-34, ROI):      1.14m [CSO, arithmetic from five-year bands] At average frequency 4:             920k net impressions-equivalent, roughly 80% of the band



Then immediately say what that does and does not mean: it is an order-of-magnitude estimate from published CPMs, it does not de-duplicate across channels, and it assumes perfect targeting which nobody gets. The honest headline is "this budget buys meaningful frequency against a tightly defined band, not national coverage."


## Step 5. The flowchart


A plan is a schedule, not a split. Build a week-by-week grid: channels down, weeks across, spend in the cells, with phases marked. Mark the launch burst, the sustain, and anything that runs always on. Note when creative changes.


Rules that keep it honest:


- A burst beats a smear at small budgets. Spreading 40,000 euro over six months reaches nobody memorably.
- Always-on search is the exception, because intent does not keep office hours.
- Build the dead periods in. Christmas, bank holidays, exam weeks, whatever is real for this audience.
- If the flight is short, say what the campaign does in week one that it does not do in week ten.


## Step 6. Dayparting, when the hour is part of the message


Some campaigns are the same at any hour. Many are not. Where the hour matters, plan it and say why: inventory price, frame of mind, competitive quiet, and the match between message and moment. Late night inventory is usually the cheapest in the market, which matters most at small budgets.


## Step 7. Measurement that can actually attribute


Decide this before the plan is bought, not after.


- **Geo phasing or a holdout.** Run two comparable regions four weeks apart, or hold one back. The difference between them is the campaign rather than the season. It costs nothing and it is the only honest way to claim an effect.
- **Pre and post tracking** on the attitude the campaign exists to change. Wave one goes in market before launch. A tracker that starts at launch measures nothing.
- **In-flight metrics** that read fast: branded search volume, direct and referral sessions, the action the work asks for.
- **The lag.** Say plainly which measures land inside the flight and which arrive afterwards. Promising an outcome inside a window it cannot appear in is how agencies lose accounts in month four.


## Step 8. Deliverables


1. **Plan on a page.** Objective, audience, working media, channel split, flight dates, the one-line reason per channel.
2. **The budget table.** Headline down to working media, as in step 1.
3. **The flowchart.** Weeks across, channels down, spend in cells.
4. **The reach and frequency table.** Channel, budget, CPM, source, impressions, estimated reach, frequency, and the caveats underneath.
5. **The measurement design.** What, when, how attributed, and what arrives after the flight.
6. **The optimisation plan.** What we review, how often, and what we are allowed to move without asking.


## Quality bar


- Does every number have a source or a stated method?
- Is working media shown as a share of the headline budget?
- Would a media director recognise the frequency assumption as deliberate?
- Can the client tell, from the flowchart alone, what is happening in week three?
- Is there a sentence saying what this budget does not buy? Including that line is what makes the rest believable.
- Run `human-pass` over the commentary. Plans attract jargon.
