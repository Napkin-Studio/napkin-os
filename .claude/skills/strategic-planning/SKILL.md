# Strategic Planning


A planner turns a business problem into a single, motivating springboard for creative work. You sit between the client and the creatives: you take a client brief that is usually a wish list, and you hand the creatives one page that gives them somewhere brilliant to go. The whole craft is compression. Take everything known about the business, the market, the product and the people, and reduce it to one insight and one proposition that a creative team could build a campaign on for years.


The spine you are working along, always in this order:


**Client brief → interrogation → the real problem → the audience → the insight → the proposition → the agency brief → hand to creatives.**


Two rules that override everything else. **Be single minded:** one problem, one audience, one insight, one proposition. The moment you have two, you have none. **Write in plain human language:** no strategist vocabulary in anything a client or a creative will read. If a line needs a glossary, rewrite it.


## Step 1: interrogate the client brief


The client brief tells you what the client thinks they want. Your job is to find what the business actually needs. Never accept the brief at face value. See `references/interrogating-the-brief.md` for the full question set. The core interrogation:


- **What is the real business problem?** Not the task ("make an ad"), the problem ("nobody under 40 considers us"). Strip the solution the client has pre decided and get back to the problem.
- **What has to change, and in whom?** Communications rarely change behaviour directly. Name the shift in what a specific group thinks, feels or does that would move the business.
- **What is the one measurable objective?** If everything is the objective, nothing is. Pick the one that matters most in the window that matters.
- **What is the barrier?** The thing in people's heads or habits stopping the business result now. The strategy usually lives in removing one barrier.
- **What can we honestly say?** What is true about the product or brand that supports the promise. You cannot brief a lie.


Reframe the problem in one sentence before you go further. A good reframe is often the whole value a planner adds.


## Step 2: get to one person (the audience)


Abstractions do not buy anything. Get from "the market" to a real person you could picture.


- Define the audience by attitude and behaviour, not just demographics. "Sole trader who treats his van as his business card" beats "men 30 to 55".
- Write the **from and to**: what this person currently thinks, feels and does, and what we need them to think, feel and do. The gap between them is the job.
- Find where they are and what frame of mind they are in when the message lands. Context is half the strategy.
- If there are genuinely two audiences, brief them separately. Do not average two people into one who does not exist.


## Step 3: find the insight


This is the hardest and most valuable step. Full method and examples in `references/insight-and-proposition.md`. The essentials:


- **An insight is not a fact, a statistic, or an observation.** It is a truth about the audience, often a tension, that when you say it out loud makes them feel understood. Facts inform; insights connect. "Vans break down" is an observation. "A tradesperson's van carries their name to every job, so a breakdown is not an inconvenience, it is a broken promise to a customer" is closer to an insight.
- Find it by immersion and by laddering. Keep asking "so what, and why does that matter to them" until you hit a human truth you could not have written from a desk.
- Look for the tension: the gap between what people say and what they do, between the category's story and lived experience, between the rational and the emotional.
- Test it: is it true, is it about the audience (not the brand), is it fresh (not a category cliché), does it give the product a role to play. If the insight does not set up the product, it is just an observation.


## Step 4: sharpen the proposition


The proposition is the single most motivating thing we can say to that person, given the insight and what is true about the product. Construction and tests in `references/insight-and-proposition.md`.


- **Single minded.** One thought. It becomes the one endline downstream, so competing propositions are a problem to solve now, not later.
- **Benefit led, from the audience's side.** Not what the product has, what it does for them. Ladder features to a functional benefit to an emotional benefit and choose the altitude that is both motivating and ownable.
- **A springboard, not a slogan.** The proposition is written for the creative team, not the public. It should make ideas fall out of it. If only one ad could come from it, it is too tight; if any ad could come from it, it is too loose.
- Test: is it single minded, true, motivating to that person, ownable by this brand and not the competitor, and does it open a space for ideas.


## Step 5: write the agency brief


One page. Written to inspire, not to cover yourself. Plain language throughout. Template and a worked example in `references/creative-brief-template.md`. The sections that earn their place:


- The business problem, in a sentence.
- The objective, the one that matters.
- The audience, as one person, with the from and to.
- The insight.
- The proposition (the one thought).
- Reasons to believe (the honest support, kept short).
- Tone of voice.
- Requirements and mandatories (media, brand line to sit under, legal, timings), kept to what genuinely constrains the idea.


The brief is a springboard, not a straitjacket. Leave the creatives room to surprise you. A brief with the idea already baked in is a failure of nerve.


## Pressure test before you hand it over


- Is the whole thing single minded, or are you smuggling in a second thought.
- Is the insight a real human truth, or a dressed up fact.
- Does the proposition motivate the person you defined, not the client.
- Could a great campaign come from this, and could a different great campaign also come from it.
- Is there a word anywhere a normal person would not use out loud. If so, rewrite.


## Anti-patterns


- The wish list brief: five objectives, three audiences, two propositions. Nothing survives contact with a creative team.
- Insight that is really a statistic or a brand fact.
- Proposition that lists features, or that is really an endline in disguise, or that no one could disagree with.
- Strategist jargon on a page a client or creative reads.
- A brief so tight it dictates the ad, or so loose it could brief anything.
- Confusing the client's stated task with the real problem.
