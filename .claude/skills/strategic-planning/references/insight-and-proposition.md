# Insight and Proposition


These are the two things a planner is actually paid for. Get them right and the brief writes itself and the creatives thank you. Get them wrong and no amount of craft downstream will save it.


## Insight


**Definition.** An insight is a truth about the audience, usually a tension, that when said out loud makes them feel understood and gives the product a role to play. It is not a fact, a statistic, or an observation. Facts inform. Insights connect.


**The ladder from fact to insight.** Keep asking "so what, and why does that matter to a real person" until you reach something you could not have written without empathy.
- Fact: vans occasionally break down.
- Observation: tradespeople need reliable vans.
- Closer: for a sole trader, the van is the business, not a tool for it.
- Insight: a tradesperson's van carries their name to every job, so letting the customer down and the van letting them down are the same fear.


**Where insights hide.**
- In the tension between what people say and what they do.
- In the gap between the category's polished story and the lived reality.
- In the emotional truth under a rational purchase (people justify with logic and buy on feeling).
- In the thing everyone in the audience knows but nobody in the category says.


**Tests for a real insight.**
- Is it true, and would the audience nod and feel seen?
- Is it about the audience, not a boast about the brand?
- Is it fresh, or a category cliché anyone could have written?
- Does it hand the product a role? An insight that does not set up the proposition is just a nice observation.


## Proposition


**Definition.** The single most motivating thing we can say to the defined audience, given the insight and what is honestly true about the product. It is written for the creative team as a springboard, and it becomes the one endline downstream.


**Construction.** Bridge the insight and a product truth into one benefit, pitched at the right altitude.
- List the relevant product truths (reasons to believe).
- Ladder each: feature, functional benefit, emotional benefit.
- Choose the altitude that is both motivating to the person and ownable by this brand. Too low (a feature) and it is forgettable and copyable; too high (a universal emotion) and it belongs to any brand in any category.
- State it as one plain thought.


**Worked example (illustrative).**
- Insight: the van carries the tradesperson's name to every job.
- Product truth: long warranty, servicing and roadside cover; genuinely dependable.
- Weak proposition (feature): "Five year warranty." Copyable, small print, no feeling.
- Weak proposition (too high): "We keep Ireland moving." Belongs to any fuel, bank or telco.
- Working proposition: "The van that works as hard for your reputation as you do." Springs many ideas, is ownable, motivates the person, and the warranty becomes the proof.


**Tests for a proposition.**
- Single minded: one thought, not a thought with an "and".
- True: supported by a reason to believe.
- Motivating: to the audience you defined, not to the client.
- Ownable: the main competitor could not put their logo on it.
- Generative: ideas fall out of it. If only one ad fits, too tight. If any ad fits, too loose.


## Single minded, or nothing


A campaign has room for one proposition, which becomes one endline. If you are holding two, the strategy is not finished. Choose, or find the higher thought that contains both. Competing endlines left in a deck are the clearest sign the strategy has not landed. Resolve them before the work goes near a client.
