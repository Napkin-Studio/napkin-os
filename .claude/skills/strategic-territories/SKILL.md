# Building strategic territories for a tissue session


A tissue session is the midpoint checkpoint between an agreed strategy and the final
pitch: you show the client a handful of early creative routes, deliberately unfinished
,  to test which thinking gains traction before anyone builds polished work. The paper
is meant to be disposable; the *thinking* is what's on trial. Done well, it de-risks the
pitch (better to learn a route dies now than on pitch day) and it lets the client feel
part of the process, which builds the chemistry that wins accounts.


Territories are the campaign iterations that all ladder up to one strategy. They are
*not* finished ads and shouldn't be presented as such, if you show polished ads, the
client buys the ad, not the team, and you lose the ability to move. Show "green shoots":
a named territory, the thought behind it, a rationale, and a few rough executional
sketches to make it tangible.


## Ladder to the client's platform, not just your proposition


Before finalising any route, find the client's **existing** brand platform, vision, or
campaign claim (it's usually in their own materials, a vision slide, an existing decal,
a live campaign, not handed to you cleanly). Every territory should feel like it extends
*that*, unless the brief explicitly asks you to challenge it. A room full of routes that
ignore the client's own platform reads as not having done the homework, and it hands a
competitor the "they actually get us" advantage.


Two checks this adds:
- **Does each territory sit comfortably under the client's own platform line?** If it
  fights the platform, either it's your deliberate challenger route (label it as such) or
  it's off-brief.
- **Does the set honour the client's stated preferences?** If the brand book says they
  want humour, warmth, a particular differentiation, at least one territory should
  visibly deliver it. A tissue set that ignores a stated brand ambition will feel off even
  when every individual route is strong.
- **Do the routes account for operational reality?** Seasonality, media timing, sales
  cycle. If most of the year's sales land in one window, show how the idea has a version
  built for that moment, a route that can't flex to the client's commercial calendar is
  weaker in the room than one that can.


## The core principle: diverge on tension, not decoration


Weak territory sets are the same idea in three colours, they differ on tone or visual
treatment but make the same strategic bet. Strong sets **diverge on where the emotional
or strategic bet is placed.** Each territory should answer the brief in a genuinely
different way, so the client's reaction *teaches* you something about what they actually
believe.


Before writing routes, list the possible *bets* the brief allows. For example, a brief
about a reliable vehicle range might permit bets on: proof/evidence, scale/momentum,
identity/pride, freedom/possibility, belonging/community. Each territory should stake a
different one. If two routes share a bet, collapse or replace one.


## How many, and what shape


Aim for **three to four** territories. Three is the workable floor for a tissue session
(fewer looks thin); four lets you include a deliberate wildcard. More than four dilutes
the room's attention and your prep quality.


A useful spread:
- **2 routes that sit closest to the brief's stated ask** (the "safe-but-strong" core).
- **1 route that reframes the brief** (same objective, unexpected angle).
- **1 wildcard**, a route that deliberately strains against a constraint in the brief.
  Its job may not be to win; it's often most valuable as a *foil* that sharpens the
  others and reveals the client's appetite for risk. Label it honestly as a wildcard.


## Anatomy of each territory


Keep each one to a tight, consistent format so they're comparable in the room:



TERRITORY NAME   , short, evocative, ownable. Not the tagline. THE THOUGHT      , one line: the strategic bet, in plain human words. RATIONALE        , 2-4 sentences: why this answers the brief, grounded in the insight and (where relevant) real data as ammunition. Say which audience(s) it serves and how. EXECUTIONAL      , 2-4 short sketches, tagged by channel (OOH / Radio / Social / SKETCHES            Digital). Lines are directional, NOT final copy. Enough to make it tangible; not so much it reads as a finished ad. VISUAL DIRECTION , a rough mock or described treatment. Rough on purpose.



Every territory must ladder back to the **same** proposition/strategy. If a route needs
a different strategy to work, it's a different brief, not a territory.


## One idea across multiple audiences


If the brief demands one idea that flexes across distinct audiences (e.g. commercial and
camper/lifestyle buyers), each territory must be shown holding *both*, ideally one
executional sketch per audience under the same thought. A territory that only works for
one audience isn't an umbrella route; note that limitation honestly, or fix it. The
strongest territories make the *same* thought feel native to very different lives.


## Grounding territories in data without becoming a data dump


Data is ammunition, not the idea. Use a hard number to make a rationale land ("X of these
people go to work on the strength of this every morning"), but the territory's *bet* is
still a human truth. A territory whose entire reason to exist is a statistic is a chart,
not a route. Lead with the human thought; deploy the number to prove it.


## Running the tissue session (guidance to give the user)


The prep is only half of it; how the room is run decides what you learn:


- **Don't ask for a flat "which is your favourite" vote.** Ask what *excites* them and
  what *worries* them about each route. The worries are where the real information is.
- **Watch for disagreement between decision-makers.** A split reaction between two client
  stakeholders is often *more* useful than consensus, it surfaces the unresolved tension
  in their own thinking, which you can then solve for on pitch day.
- **Listen for "the bits that never made it to the brief."** A great tissue leaves you
  understanding the client better than the written brief ever told you.
- **Wrap with a summary the client confirms out loud.** Recap what you heard and what
  they'll see next, and get explicit agreement, so the next meeting starts from alignment
  rather than re-litigation.
- **Protect the bold route.** Tissue sessions can institutionalise caution, the safe
  route gains momentum just by being discussed. If you believe in the brave one, give it
  enough air to be understood before the room's risk-aversion buries it.


## Self-check before delivering


- Do any two territories share the same strategic bet? → collapse or replace.
- Does every route ladder to the client's *existing* platform (not just your proposition)? → if a route fights their platform, label it the challenger or cut it.
- Does at least one route honour the client's stated brand preferences (e.g. humour, a named differentiation)? → if none do, you've missed a soft mandatory.
- Can the idea flex to the client's commercial calendar (seasonality, key sales window)? → show the version built for the big moment.
- Is any "territory" actually a finished ad? → roughen it; show the thinking, not the artwork.
- Does every route ladder to the one proposition? → if not, it's off-strategy.
- For multi-audience briefs: does each route visibly hold both audiences? → fix or flag.
- Is the wildcard labelled honestly, with its job (often: foil) stated? → say so.
- Could a creative team actually build each one? → if it's just a slogan, it's not a route.


## Output


Present territories as a comparable set, same structure each, rough by design. If
building a visual room-map document or deck, read the relevant document/visual skill
first. Make clear throughout that nothing is final: the purpose is to narrow toward the
client's answer, not to present finished work.
